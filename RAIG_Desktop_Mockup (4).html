California's Untold Stories
History of Marginalization in California
Understanding the roots of harm that reparations work seeks to address

California's history is inseparable from the systematic dispossession, exclusion, and exploitation of communities of color. Reparative justice requires an honest reckoning with these histories — and a commitment to repair. Below are four communities whose stories are central to California's past and to our advocacy.

Native Americans

Pre-Contact Through Present

California was home to the most diverse concentration of Indigenous peoples in North America. Spanish colonization brought the mission system, which forced labor, cultural erasure, and mass death. After U.S. statehood, state-funded militias carried out massacres, and federal policies removed survivors to distant reservations. The Indian Indenture Act of 1850 legalized Native child labor. Land allotment policies stripped tribal sovereignty and communal holdings.

  • Estimated 300,000+ Indigenous people in California before Spanish contact
  • Population fell to ~15,000 by 1900 due to genocide and disease
  • State-sanctioned "Indian Wars" funded through 1870s

African Americans

From Statehood Through Redlining and Beyond

Though California entered the Union as a free state in 1850, Black Californians faced systemic exclusion from the start. Anti-Black testimony laws denied legal standing in courts. The Great Migration brought hundreds of thousands to California, only to find racially restrictive housing covenants, redlining by the Federal Housing Administration, and urban renewal demolitions targeting Black neighborhoods. Oakland's West Oakland, Los Angeles's Crenshaw, and Compton were transformed by displacement policies. California's Task Force to Study and Develop Reparation Proposals, established in 2020, documented these harms.

  • FHA redlining maps covered major California cities through the 1980s
  • Urban renewal demolished Black neighborhoods across the state 1950s–70s
  • California Reparations Task Force issued its final report in 2023

Mexican Americans

Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo Through Present

The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promised citizenship and land rights to Californios — promises quickly broken. The California Land Act of 1851 forced Mexican landowners into years of costly litigation; most lost their ranchos. During the Great Depression, over one million people of Mexican descent — many U.S. citizens — were forcibly deported in "repatriation drives." Bracero Program workers faced wage theft and unsafe conditions. Agricultural laborers faced systemic exclusion from New Deal labor protections.

  • ~13,000 Californios dispossessed of land grants after 1851
  • ~400,000 Californians deported during 1930s repatriation drives
  • Bracero Program: 1942–1964, ~4.6 million contracts issued nationwide

Japanese Americans

Alien Land Laws Through WWII Incarceration

California's Alien Land Laws (1913, 1920) prohibited Japanese immigrants from owning or leasing agricultural land. Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, Executive Order 9066 led to the forced incarceration of over 120,000 people of Japanese descent — two-thirds of them U.S. citizens — in concentration camps. Families lost farms, businesses, homes, and savings. California was home to the largest Japanese American population affected. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 acknowledged the injustice and provided limited reparations.

  • 120,000+ incarcerated, primarily from California, Oregon, and Washington
  • Estimated $6 billion in 2024 dollars lost in property and income
  • 1988: $20,000 and formal apology issued to survivors
